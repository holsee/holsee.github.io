Some years ago I built a near-duplicate detection service at work, and it's the piece of my work there I most enjoy explaining, because the interesting parts are all portable and the best bit ended up open source. So let me tell it the way it's worth telling: the problem, the idea, the one optimisation that mattered, and the library, [spirit_fingers](https://github.com/holsee/spirit_fingers), that outlived the service and has the receipts to prove it works.

## The problem a checksum can't see

The setting is document uploads at scale: coursework submissions. There was already duplicate detection, and it worked the obvious way: hash the file, and if you've seen that hash before, it's a duplicate. That catches the lazy case perfectly: the identical file, submitted twice.

It catches nothing else. Change a single word. Add a trailing space. Re-export the same document to PDF so the bytes shuffle. Every one of those produces a completely different checksum, because that is exactly what a checksum is *for*: a cryptographic hash has the avalanche property, where flipping one input bit flips about half the output bits. Two documents that differ by a comma look as unrelated to a checksum as two documents that share nothing. So the near-identical resubmission, the one you actually care about, sails straight through a checksum-based check looking brand new.

That's the gap. The requirement isn't "are these files identical", it's "are these documents *nearly* the same", and those are different questions that want different tools.

## The idea: hash for similarity, not identity

The tool I reached for is locality-sensitive hashing, and it's the inversion of everything a checksum does. Where a cryptographic hash is designed so similar inputs give wildly different outputs, an LSH is designed so *similar inputs give similar outputs*. Fingerprint each document with one, and two near-identical documents land at nearly the same fingerprint. "Nearly the same document" becomes "nearly the same number", and now you can measure it.

The specific scheme is SimHash. Reduce a document to a 64-bit fingerprint built so that similar text produces fingerprints a small **Hamming distance** apart: a small count of differing bits. Compare two documents by counting the bits that differ: zero means identical, a handful means a near-duplicate, a lot means unrelated. The whole detection collapses to "fingerprint the upload, and find any stored fingerprint within *N* bits of it", with *N* a threshold you can dial: tight for "basically the same submission", looser for "suspiciously similar". An integer distance instead of an opaque similarity score, which means the knob is honest: there's a number of bits, not a black box.

### How the fingerprint gets built

A SimHash fingerprint is a vote. Split the document into features (words, say) and hash each feature to 64 bits. Then, for every one of those 64 bit positions, tally the features: a `1` in that position votes **+1**, a `0` votes **−1**. Why does this make *similar* documents land *close*? Because each output bit is a majority vote, and one feature rarely overturns a column. Change a word, and only the columns that word touched can flip, and usually none of them do. Share most of your features and you share most of your bits, which is exactly the property a checksum refuses to give you.

### Seeing it in Elixir

That's the whole library surface: fingerprint a string, then measure two fingerprints against each other. [spirit_fingers](https://github.com/holsee/spirit_fingers) does the hashing in Rust and hands you plain integers:

```elixir
# two submissions that differ by a single word
iex> {:ok, a} = SpiritFingers.SimHash.similarity_hash("The cat sat on the mat")
{:ok, 2595200813813010837}
iex> {:ok, b} = SpiritFingers.SimHash.similarity_hash("The cat sat under the mat")
{:ok, 2595269945604666783}

# the two fingerprints differ in only 10 of their 64 bits
iex> SpiritFingers.SimHash.hamming_distance(a, b)
{:ok, 10}

# an unrelated sentence lands much further away
iex> {:ok, c} = SpiritFingers.SimHash.similarity_hash("Why the lucky stiff")
{:ok, 1155526875459215761}
iex> SpiritFingers.SimHash.hamming_distance(a, c)
{:ok, 21}

# similarity/2 does the whole round trip and normalises to 0.0-1.0
iex> SpiritFingers.SimHash.similarity("Hocus pocus", "Hocus pocus pilatus pas")
{:ok, 0.9375}
```

One word changed moves ten bits out of sixty-four; a sentence about something else moves twenty-one. The detection threshold sits in the gap between "a near-duplicate" and "a different document", and you set it in units you can actually reason about: bits.

### Comparing two fingerprints

The comparison is the cheapest part, and it's pure bit arithmetic: **XOR the two fingerprints, then count the ones.** Every position where the two fingerprints agree cancels to `0`; every position where they differ leaves a `1`; and the number of `1`s left over is the Hamming distance. That's it. `hamming_distance/2` is an XOR and a population count (one machine instruction each on any modern CPU), which is the other half of why this scales to millions of documents: fingerprinting is the expensive step you do once per document, and comparison is nearly free.

The service is otherwise unremarkable (it ingests uploads, fingerprints them, and flags the near-duplicates), and I'll leave the internals there, because the internals aren't the interesting part. The interesting part is what happens when you have to do this millions of times.

## The one optimisation that mattered

Fingerprinting is the hot path, and this has to run over millions of documents, some of them large. That scale is where the naive approach dies. SimHash means hashing every feature of a document and accumulating bit weights across all of them: tight, repetitive bit-twiddling over what can be a very large binary. That is precisely the kind of work the BEAM is *bad* at. Elixir is wonderful for the service around it; it is the wrong place to sit in a hot loop shifting bits through a megabyte of text.

So the fingerprinting moved into Rust, behind a NIF, via Rustler. The service stays Elixir (supervision, the API, the storage) and calls into a small Rust function for the one thing Elixir shouldn't be doing. Right tool, right layer: the orchestration on the BEAM, the number-crunching in native code, a clean boundary between them.

## The library that came out of it: spirit_fingers

That Rust NIF didn't stay locked inside the employer's service. It's a general, reusable thing (SimHash for Elixir), so I extracted it and open-sourced it on my own time as [spirit_fingers](https://github.com/holsee/spirit_fingers). The service depends on it; the library is mine. (I've written separately about [seven years of maintaining that NIF](/spirit-fingers-seven-years-of-a-rust-nif/).)

Open-sourcing it also meant the performance claim could stop being something you take on my word: there's a [benchmark suite on GitHub](https://github.com/holsee/simhash_benchmarks) that runs spirit_fingers against the other SimHash libraries in the Elixir ecosystem. The numbers and their caveats live in [the spirit_fingers post](/spirit-fingers-seven-years-of-a-rust-nif/); the short version is that on a short string the Rust NIF is a couple of hundred times faster and four orders of magnitude lighter on memory, and on a megabyte document it's the only one of the three that finishes at all, fingerprinting 11.5 MB in about 73 milliseconds where one pure-Elixir library errors and the other climbs past 5 GB of RAM. At millions-of-documents scale that last property is the one that decided it.

## What travels

Two things, and neither is specific to coursework or to the employer. When the question is *similarity* rather than *identity*, a checksum is the wrong instrument and locality-sensitive hashing is the right one: turn "are these alike" into a distance you can threshold. And when the hot path is tight numeric work over big binaries at scale, that work belongs in Rust behind a NIF, with the BEAM doing what the BEAM is good at around it. The bonus lesson is the one I'd press hardest: the reusable core of a problem you solve at work is often worth lifting out into the open, where it gets a life, and a benchmark, of its own.
